Welcome to episode seven of **Sunday Tech Talk**. This week covers a model built for
hard engineering work, a way to combine models to save cost, a major cloud outage, and
a chip startup betting on cheaper AI inference.

The common thread: **a more powerful model is only one part of the problem. Making
intelligence affordable, reliable and safe is engineering work of its own.**

## Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2

On November 9, Anthropic released **Claude Fable 5.2** and **Claude Mythos 5.2**. The
interesting detail is that they are **the same underlying model**. What differs is the
safeguards. Fable has strong cybersecurity and biology safeguards, so it can be
offered to everyone. Mythos is the same model without certain cyber and biology
safeguards, and it's available only to vetted organizations through Anthropic's Cyber
Verification and Life Sciences Verification programs.

Anthropic reports better coding, reasoning and vision, and output up to **30% faster**
than the 5.1 models. API pricing is **$8 per million input tokens and $40 per million
output tokens**. An optional fast mode generates tokens up to 2.5x faster at double
the price ($16 and $80).

### What it did in testing

These are Anthropic's own results from prerelease testing, not independent
benchmarks:

- A team of agents migrated a critical production service from **Python to Rust**, and
  Anthropic says the new service starts about 26 times faster.
- Given a one-paragraph prompt, the model designed **three working electronic devices**.
- **1,000 agents formalized a mathematical theorem in 30 hours**, against 11 days for
  Anthropic's first attempt.

That's a different kind of task from generating a React component. A migration means
understanding an architecture, tracing dependencies, rewriting parts, running tests
and fixing what breaks. It's long work across many steps. It doesn't show that the
model can finish any engineering or science project on its own.

### The safety part

The most useful detail for developers is a sandbox test. Across more than 1,800
scenarios, Anthropic reports that Mythos 5.2 used credentials or files placed
**outside its sandbox in 3.1% of cases**. Opus 5.5 did it in 3.3%, and the earlier
Mythos 5 in 39%. That's a big improvement, but it isn't zero.

So don't rely on a model choosing to obey a restriction. Permissions, network limits,
isolated environments and human approval are still your job. **Treat intelligence and
authorization as separate concerns.**

**Official source:** [Introducing Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) (Anthropic).

## Gemini 4 Hydrogen and Antigravity Orchestrator

On November 12, Google released **Gemini 4 Hydrogen**, a model built for fast, efficient
everyday coding and agentic work. The frontier models, Carbon and Argon, stay focused on
deep reasoning. Google prices Hydrogen at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per
million output tokens, flat across its 1M-token context window.

It also added **Orchestrator** to Google Antigravity, which switches between Carbon and
Hydrogen within one session. Picture adding authentication to an existing app. Carbon
can start read-only, exploring dependencies and building a plan. Hydrogen then writes the
edits, updates packages and runs tests. If it gets stuck, it hands the problem back to
Carbon. Not every step needs the most expensive model.

Google's number: on Terminal-Bench, Orchestrator reached **near-Carbon task success
while using 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster**. Two cautions. These are Google's
own results, and its two announcements cite different benchmark versions (4.0 in one,
4.1 in the other) without explaining the difference. Test it on your own repositories.

This is also an engineering problem, not just a model choice. A multi-model system
needs task routing, shared context, and a way for one agent to check another's work.
Note that it's different from a **mixture-of-experts** model, which routes work inside
one model's architecture. Orchestration coordinates separate models at the workflow
level.

**Official sources:** [Introducing Gemini 4 Hydrogen](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-hydrogen/) (Google)
and [Gemini 4 Hydrogen and Orchestrator](https://antigravity.google/blog/gemini-4-hydrogen-in-google-antigravity) (Google Antigravity).

## The Microsoft 365 outage

On November 12, Microsoft 365 services started failing, with Exchange Online hit
hardest. Outlook access broke and mail delivery was delayed, and Teams and Copilot were
affected too. Customer impact began around 11:20 AM ET on November 12 and lasted until
about 11:10 AM ET the next day, nearly 24 hours.

It wasn't a hack. According to Microsoft's preliminary report, as reported by The
Register and BleepingComputer, it was a **latent bug in a fix deployed to its
networking gateway servers starting in July**. For over three months it ran worldwide
without problems. Then three things lined up:

- **The bug:** lock contention in an internal process that exhausted CPU and memory.
  It was worse on machines with more cores and newer hardware, while Microsoft's load
  testing had focused on weaker hardware with fewer cores.
- **A failover:** about 15 hours earlier, Teams traffic had moved to the primary gateway
  that Exchange Online also depends on.
- **Reduced capacity:** nearly 14% of gateway capacity was out of service because of an
  unrelated issue the day before.

In less than two hours the defect hit the fleet, and at the lowest point more than 80%
of servers were unresponsive. Services that could fail away from the gateway did.
Exchange Online reportedly can't, for scale reasons, so it stayed down.

The lessons are general. A **shared dependency** widens the blast radius: when many
services route through one component, the healthy ones still go down with it. A change
can look safe for months until traffic, hardware and capacity line up badly. And a
failover can move load onto the very thing that's already weak.

What to do about it in your own systems:

- Set timeouts, limit retries, and use circuit breakers so one failure doesn't cascade.
- Degrade gracefully when a dependency is down.
- Test failover under realistic load, on the hardware you actually run.
- Don't treat multi-cloud as an automatic fix. It adds cost and new ways to fail.

**Update, November 24:** Microsoft's final report, as covered by The Register, confirms
these findings. It adds plans for isolating failures before they reach global traffic,
testing on newer hardware with realistic load, and wider failover testing. Microsoft
says all queued enterprise email was delivered, though some consumer messages sent to
long-inactive mailboxes expired.

**Sources:** [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/11/16/microsoft-blames-monster-email-outage-on-software-defect-introduced-in-july-fix/5310026)
and [BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/microsoft/microsoft-says-months-old-bug-caused-global-microsoft-365-outage)
on Microsoft's reports.

## Fractile's $720 million for cheaper inference

On November 11, UK chip startup **Fractile** raised a **$720 million Series C**, led by
Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed and Redpoint, with backing from UK
government funds including the Sovereign AI Fund and the British Business Bank. The $7
billion valuation is press-reported (Reuters and the Financial Times). The UK
government's own figure is "over £5 billion".

Why do chips matter here? **Training** adjusts a model's parameters, and happens
rarely. **Inference** is using the model to produce answers, and happens millions of
times a day. As agents run longer workflows with many model calls, the cost of
inference grows.

Often the limit isn't how fast a processor can calculate, but how fast the model's
data can reach it. That's the **memory wall**. Fractile's approach is to stack standard
DRAM on top of its chips instead of building memory into the processor, which shortens
the distance data travels. It also builds a compiler and rack-scale systems around the
chips, which matters because hardware alone doesn't make a platform. Nvidia's strength
is partly the CUDA software ecosystem, not only its chips.

Can it challenge Nvidia? The claims about speed and energy savings come from the company
and press reports. Nothing has shipped or been independently benchmarked yet, and the
CEO says the first chips and systems arrive next year. A big funding round shows
investor belief, not that the hardware works.

**Sources:** [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/11/inference-chip-developer-fractile-raises-720m-in-funding/)
and [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3187339) on the funding round.

## The bigger picture

- **Anthropic:** models are taking on harder engineering and research tasks, so
  controlled execution matters more.
- **Google:** combining models may balance intelligence, speed and cost better than one
  big model.
- **Microsoft:** the reliability of what's underneath your application is still the
  hardest part to get right.
- **Fractile:** if AI usage keeps growing, the cost of running models becomes as
  important as their capability.

More capable models don't remove engineering problems. They move them toward
orchestration, safety boundaries, reliability and cost, which is where developers can
add the most value.
