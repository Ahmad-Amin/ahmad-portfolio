Welcome to episode five of **Sunday Tech Talk**. This week I have one thing I built,
and three stories about security: a record crypto exchange incident, an AI model that
can find and fix vulnerabilities, and a poisoned developer extension.

The theme is simple: **as software gets more powerful and automated, the systems
behind it matter more.** Let's start with something personal.

## FinScan AI: making expense tracking less tedious

Tracking expenses is easy to start and hard to keep up. You buy groceries, pay for
fuel, order food, send money to someone, and by the end of the week some of it
is forgotten, because every entry means opening an app and typing.

I built **FinScan AI** to remove as much of that typing as possible. It's on Google
Play and captures spending from two places you already have:

- **Transaction notifications.** Banks tell you about every purchase, either by SMS or
  through their own app's notifications. With your permission, FinScan AI reads both
  kinds of alert and turns them into expense records without you opening the app.
- **Receipt scanning.** Take a photo of a receipt and AI extracts the merchant,
  amount, date and category. That helps for cash purchases, which don't generate a
  bank alert.

Around that it has multiple wallets, spending analytics, budgets with alerts before
you overspend, multi-currency support and a one-time premium purchase instead of
a subscription.

Both methods have engineering limits worth being honest about. Banks word their
messages differently, and reading SMS and app notifications depends on the
permissions a user grants (and on how Android behaves on their device). A
receipt can be blurry, laid out strangely, or missing a field, and the app has to tell
the final total from taxes and individual line items. AI helps with that variety,
but extracted data should still be checked, so you can review every transaction.

The lesson I keep coming back to: **good software doesn't always need a new
behavior. Sometimes it just makes an existing one easier.**

## Binance's $6.3 billion incident: what we know so far

Binance reported a major security incident involving unauthorized withdrawals,
announced on October 30. These are the figures from Binance's own updates, and its
full technical report isn't out yet:

- The estimated loss is about **$6.3 billion** in tokens.
- Binance says it will absorb the loss, not its users, using its SAFU emergency fund
  and company funds. It says it has set aside more than $6.34 billion.
- About **$3 billion** has been frozen on-chain.
- Withdrawals were paused, then restarted in phases on November 1. Coverage differs on
  how much of the loss had been replaced at that point (Binance says 90%, Reuters
  reports 95%).

### How did it happen?

Binance's first update said the incident **may** involve a compromised laptop
belonging to a DevOps engineer, which could have given attackers access to a service
container to submit unauthorized withdrawals. A later update said further investigation
had uncovered **additional points of breach**, and that the full attack sequence is
still being established. Both updates say Binance found **no evidence that wallet
private keys leaked.**

That detail is worth pausing on.

### Stealing keys vs controlling the signing system

Cryptocurrency moves when a transaction is signed with a private key. There are two
very different ways an attacker can cause a theft:

1. **Steal the private key.** They can sign transactions themselves.
2. **Compromise a system that's already allowed to ask for signatures.** They never
   see the key, but they can get fraudulent transactions signed.

Think of a simplified withdrawal flow: the API authenticates the customer, a risk
engine checks limits and suspicious activity, a signing service signs approved
requests, and the signed transaction is broadcast. If an attacker controls a trusted
component in that chain, protecting the key alone isn't enough. Binance hasn't said
that's what happened here, which is exactly why the post-mortem matters, but it's
the lesson to take from the reported shape of the incident.

### What engineers can take from this

- **Least privilege:** laptops, service containers and accounts should hold only the
  access they need.
- **Authorization isn't implied by access:** a service being able to request a
  signature shouldn't mean every request is valid.
- **Independent controls:** limits, extra approvals and monitoring for large or
  unusual transfers.
- **Isolation:** compromising one developer machine shouldn't give a path into
  production systems that move money.

This applies well beyond crypto, to payments, banking and any system with a trusted
approval path. **You can have excellent cryptography and still fail if the
infrastructure around it is compromised.**

**Official sources:** Binance's [incident update](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/c5a8729a97ac486d963784d96c5642d7)
and [October 30 follow-up](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/d3306d9dca5243548c813b605e1d1751).

## Gemini 4 Carbon: an AI model that finds and fixes vulnerabilities

On October 28, Google announced **Gemini 4 Carbon**, which it calls its strongest
model, with state-of-the-art cybersecurity and long-horizon abilities. It builds on
the model I covered in [episode one](/blog/sunday-tech-talk-1), Gemini 4 Argon.

Google reported three cybersecurity results:

| Benchmark | Google-reported score | What it tests |
|---|---:|---|
| SEC-bench Pro | 88.2% | Finding real vulnerabilities |
| ExploitGym | 54.8% | Validating that a flaw is exploitable |
| CWE-bench | 71.0% | Patching the root cause |

These are Google's own numbers, on specific benchmarks, evaluated without
guardrails. They show the capability, not how it performs on your codebase.

What's interesting is the combination. Traditional scanners flag suspicious patterns.
An agent can, in principle, go further: trace how input reaches a vulnerable line, check
whether the path is actually reachable, validate the issue in a safe test
environment, propose a fix and write a regression test. Google also says Carbon has
worked through problems for days internally. That's an internal claim, not a guarantee
for every production setting.

### Why access is limited

A model that helps defenders find vulnerabilities could help attackers too. Google
says it's starting with a subset of cyber defenders, then adding trusted partners
through its Fairwind program, before a wider release, with no date given. It also says
it's taking part in the US government's voluntary pre-release testing.

Is restricted access the right call? Broad access could help smaller organizations,
and restricted access lowers some misuse, but it also limits who benefits. It's a hard
trade-off, and this won't be the last model to raise it.

For developers, the opportunity isn't just more code, it's better, more secure code.
But AI-generated patches can miss the root cause or add regressions, so tests, review
and human approval still matter. **The goal is faster, more reliable security
engineering, not blind trust in generated fixes.**

**Official source:** [Introducing Gemini 4 Carbon](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-carbon/) (Google).

## A tampered Claude Code extension on Cursor's marketplace

The last story hits the tools developers use. On October 29, Cursor disclosed that
tampered versions of the Claude Code extension, **2.1.312 and 2.1.313** for macOS and
Windows, had been available in **Cursor's extension marketplace** for roughly half a
day. Cursor estimated that at most 5,000 users were affected.

Anthropic confirmed to Microsoft that its systems weren't compromised and that it
didn't build the tampered package. Microsoft found no evidence it reached the Visual
Studio Marketplace or npm. This was a marketplace distribution problem, not a breach
of Anthropic. Neither company has said how the attacker got into the marketplace.

### Why extensions are risky

An IDE extension runs code on your machine, often with access to your files,
processes, credentials and network. A developer laptop typically holds GitHub access,
SSH keys, cloud sessions, `.env` files and routes into production, so it's an
attractive target.

### What the tampered extension did

According to Cursor and Microsoft's analysis:

- **It collected host details,** including hostname, username, OS and the global Git
  email, and listed the *names* of environment variables that look like secrets
  (not their values).
- **It tried to add an attacker's SSH key** (labelled `svc-telemetry`) to the user's
  `authorized_keys` file, which lists the keys allowed to log in over SSH.
- **It tried to open a reverse SSH tunnel.** Normally you connect out from your
  machine to a server. A reverse tunnel makes your machine connect *out* to the
  attacker's server and expose your local SSH port back through that connection,
  which is a handy admin technique and a well-known way to create a hidden way in.
- **It set up persistence** with a macOS LaunchAgent, a Windows scheduled task or, on
  Linux, a cron job, so it would run again after a restart. Microsoft notes the names
  were chosen to look like legitimate monitoring components.

### Did it work?

Partly. Cursor says the step that enables SSH on the machine needs admin rights the
extension usually lacks, so on most machines the attacker probably couldn't log in.
But Cursor also says the extension **did** add the attacker's key, install
persistence and send host details to the attacker's server.

### What to do if you installed it

- **Check for the `svc-telemetry` key** in `authorized_keys` (Cursor and Microsoft
  published exact commands).
- **Uninstalling isn't enough,** because the payload leaves behind its own scripts and
  startup entries. If you find signs, isolate the machine and consider rebuilding it.
- **Rotate credentials and revoke sessions** reachable from that machine, working
  from a clean one.
- **Longer term:** limit extensions to approved publishers and consider a short
  waiting period before installing newly published versions, as Microsoft suggests.

Together with the LiteLLM attack in [episode four](/blog/sunday-tech-talk-4), it's
the same lesson: **the channels you install software from are security boundaries
too.**

**Official sources:** Cursor's [security notice](https://cursor.com/blog/claude-code-extension-security-notice)
and [Microsoft's technical analysis](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/10/30/inside-malicious-claude-code-branded-cursor-extension-persistent-ssh-backdoor/).

## The bigger picture

- **FinScan AI:** automation makes everyday software more useful.
- **Binance:** secure the authorization path, not just the keys.
- **Gemini 4 Carbon:** AI is getting good at finding and fixing vulnerabilities, and
  access has to be handled carefully.
- **The Cursor extension:** the tools developers trust are targets.

**Building powerful software is only one part of engineering. Making it secure,
reliable and trustworthy matters just as much**, and more so as AI joins the
development workflow.
